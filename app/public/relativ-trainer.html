Gender comes from the main clause, case comes from the relative clause.
Three questions decide every relative pronoun. Learn the order and the case stops being guesswork.
Gender from the main clause. Case from the relative clause.

That is the whole trick. Almost every mistake in a relative clause comes from mixing up two separate decisions: the gender (der/die/das) is decided by the noun in the main clause, the case (nominative/accusative/dative/genitive) is decided by the job the pronoun does inside the relative clause. Two different sentences, two different questions. In German: Das Genus kommt aus dem Hauptsatz, der Kasus aus dem Relativsatz.

The three-step method

Every relative pronoun comes out of three questions, always in this order. Step 3 overrules step 2.

  1. Which word is being described? → gender and number

    Find the antecedent (das Bezugswort) right before the comma. Its grammatical gender and its number move into the pronoun. The case of the antecedent itself is completely irrelevant.

    Ich habe einen Freund (accusative), der … ← Freund is masculine, and that is all you take from it.

  2. What job does it do inside the relative clause? → case

    The two-sentence test: turn the relative clause into a sentence of its own and put the antecedent back into it. Whatever case it gets there is the case of the pronoun.

    der Kollege, ___ ich geholfen habe → „Ich habe dem Kollegen geholfen." → dative → dem

  3. Is there a preposition in front? → then it decides

    A preposition always stands before the pronoun and overrules every other rule. With two-way prepositions: wo? (where?) → dative, wohin? (where to?) → accusative.

    der Job, auf den ich warte (warten auf + acc.) · die Stadt, in der ich wohne (wo?) · die Stadt, in die ich ziehe (wohin?)

  4. Then: comma, verb to the end, comma

    A relative clause is a subordinate clause. It sits directly behind its antecedent, the conjugated verb goes right to the end, and after the closing comma the main clause carries on.

    Der Zug, der um 8 Uhr abfährt, ist der ICE.

The three worlds

A relative clause starts with exactly one of three words. Which one is decided by the kind of antecedent, not by the case.

der · die · dasThe normal case: a real noun

Das ist der Kollege, der mir geholfen hat, den ich gestern getroffen habe, dem ich vertraue und dessen Frau bei uns arbeitet.

That's the colleague who helped me, whom I met yesterday, whom I trust and whose wife works with us.

Use this whenever a real noun is being described. Gender from the noun, case from the job it does. With a preposition, the preposition comes first.

der / den / dem / dessendie / der / derendenen
wasNo noun — or the whole sentence

Alles, was ich weiß, nichts, was du versprochen hast, das Beste, was mir passieren konnte — und: Er hat bestanden, was mich sehr gefreut hat.

Everything I know, nothing you promised, the best thing that could happen to me — and: he passed, which made me very happy.

Three triggers: an indefinite pronoun (nichts, alles, etwas, vieles, weniges, einiges, manches, das) · a nominalised superlative (das Beste, das Schönste) · a reference to the entire statement.

nichts / alles / etwasdas Bestewhole sentence

But: never was for people — „jemand, der …", „niemand, dem …". And after a real noun it stays der/die/das: „das Buch, das ich lese".

wo · wo(r)+prepPlaces — and sentence reference with a preposition

Salzburg, wo Mozart geboren wurde. Leipzig, wohin ich gezogen bin. Die Regierung macht vieles, worüber man sich aufregen kann.

Salzburg, where Mozart was born. Leipzig, where I moved to. The government does a lot you can get worked up about.

Two situations: with places, wo / wohin / woher and preposition + pronoun are equally good — but after names of towns and countries only wo/wohin/woher works. When the clause refers to the whole statement and the verb needs a preposition, it becomes wo(r) + preposition.

Two shortcuts for C1

wer / wen / wem — the person with no name

When no particular person is meant, wer replaces the whole phrase "the one who". The case follows the job the person does inside the clause, and the verb still goes to the end.

Wer noch Urlaub hat, muss ihn nehmen. · Wem das Essen nicht schmeckt, (der) soll sich melden.

Participial attributes — the compressed relative clause

Written German squeezes the whole relative clause in front of the noun. Participle I = active and in progress, participle II = passive or completed.

die Temperaturen, die steigen → die steigenden Temperaturen
das Grundwasser, das verschmutzt wurde → das verschmutzte Grundwasser
